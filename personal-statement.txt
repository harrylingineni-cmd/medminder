At the KSN national mathematics finals in 2021, I learned how quickly confidence can become a liability. I was the first student from SMP Katolik Stella Maris Tomohon to reach that stage, but I let that achievement make me overconfident. After losing, I considered quitting. My mathematics teacher told me that perfection belongs only to God. I took that to mean a ranking could show me where to improve without deciding whether I belonged. I kept competing.

That distinction matters to why I want to study computer science. I want confidence to come from understanding how something works, not from assuming I am good enough to make it work. Mathematics gives me problems to reason through; programming lets me turn that reasoning into something another person can actually use.

I taught myself Lua to script UI widgets for Beyond All Reason. In Project Zomboid, I built Global Occult Coalition Radio, an in-game radio mod published on Steam Workshop. It has received 41 ratings. That number does not tell me everything about its quality, but it means the project has reached people beyond my own screen. I also worked with a collaborator on MediGuard, a health-tech application built using Flutter and Dart and published on Google Play. These projects are concrete reasons I want to keep studying computing, rather than simply subjects I enjoy reading about.

A robotics competition gave me a less tidy test of that interest. I designed and hand-soldered a PCB using Arduino parts, without a kit or reference design. There was flux residue, components did not fit neatly, and the positive and negative terminals were uncomfortably close. A jury member asked whether it would short-circuit. I was confident because I had traced every connection myself. The build, held together with tape, worked.

Still, working once is not the same as being well engineered. Looking back, I would not defend the cramped layout just because the circuit ran. The experience makes my next step clear: I want the theoretical grounding to judge my designs beyond whether they function. My teacher had challenged confidence based on a result; this circuit gave me another reason to question it.

CUHK-Shenzhen's Computer Science and Engineering programme appeals to me because it combines a mathematical foundation with applications in machine learning, artificial intelligence and data science. Across six semesters, my mathematics scores were 98–99 and informatics scores 95–100; my STEM average was 97.87. I also earned an Edexcel IGCSE Mathematics Grade 8. I want to put that preparation to work in the optional Artificial Intelligence stream, learning how mathematical reasoning supports models whose results need careful evaluation.

My current Titanic project on Kaggle, under the username Nekkixn, is unfinished. Next, I would like to explore water-quality classification, drawing on my experience keeping koi and helping manage our family orchard. I have not built that project yet. It is a question I want to investigate, not an achievement I can claim.

What attracts me is the move from writing game behaviour to asking what a model can learn from data. I want to understand how to choose inputs, test predictions and recognise when a result is misleading. For a water-quality project, that means being clear about what available measurements can support, rather than presenting a classification as a complete judgement about whether water is safe for living things in practical settings.

At CUHK-Shenzhen, I want to become more exacting about what I build and what I say it can do. I do not need every attempt to be perfect. I do need to understand its weaknesses well enough to make the next version better.
